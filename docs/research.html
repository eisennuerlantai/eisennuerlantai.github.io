Research

RESEARCH AGENDA

I study how the distribution of firms (their size, network position, profitability, and pricing power) shapes aggregate prices and profits.

My dissertation, The Granular and Network Origins of Inflation in the United States, traces cost shocks from individual firms through production networks into producer prices. A second line of work examines profitability and accumulation from the perspective of classical political economy, treating profitability as a distribution across heterogeneous firms rather than a single aggregate rate. Methodologically, I rely on firm-level data, input-output networks, and Bayesian hierarchical models that estimate distributions of responses rather than averages.

Job Market Paper

Industrial Structure and the Transmission of Granular Cost Shocks

with Ellis Scharfenaker · September 2026

We examine how firm-level cost shocks are transmitted into industry producer prices in the United States. Using Compustat data, NAICS3 producer price indexes, and input-output linkages, we construct granular industry cost shocks and upstream exposure measures. Bayesian hierarchical local projections show that pass-through is positive and persistent, but highly uneven across sectors. Price transmission is concentrated in upstream and more concentrated industries, where network position and pricing power make cost shocks more likely to propagate into producer prices. During the pandemic, pass-through did not rise uniformly; it intensified in a small set of upstream, energy-related sectors. Aggregating the estimated industry-level transmission mechanism with sales weights, we show that the heterogeneous pass-through structure accounts for a meaningful share of covered-industry inflation, particularly during episodes dominated by large upstream cost disturbances.

Inflation, Pricing, and Industrial Structure

Granularity, Networks, and Aggregate Price Volatility [PDF]

with Ellis Scharfenaker · Revise and resubmit, Economics Letters

Abstract

Idiosyncratic firm-level cost shocks affect aggregate price volatility when they survive aggregation within industries and propagate through the production network. We show that when firm shocks are demeaned within industries and aggregated with lagged sales weights, the relevant concentration term includes a finite-firm correction to the Herfindahl index. Under linear network propagation, the variance of the granular-network component of inflation is proportional to a statistic combining within-industry concentration and network influence. Using Compustat firm data, BLS producer price indexes, and BEA input-output tables, we find that this component is positively related to realized producer-price inflation and that granular-network volatility concentrates in a handful of industries, led by petroleum and coal products.

Profitability and Accumulation

Measuring Marxian Categories in the U.S. Economy: Profitability, Distribution, and Capital Composition [PDF]

with Minqi Li · Working paper, September 2026

Abstract

Empirical work on the rate of profit depends on how theoretical categories are mapped into national accounts. Building on the framework of Shaikh and Tonak, we classify every industry in the U.S. input-output accounts by its role in social reproduction and construct the Marxian net product, surplus value, the rate of surplus value, the organic composition of capital, and four measures of the rate of profit for 2017–2022. Only 56–57 percent of the net product is recorded in production sectors, while about 40 percent is recorded in business sectors that realize output. The level of the rate of profit ranges from 9 to 53 percent depending on the treatment of unproductive labor and capital, although all measures move together over time. An exact decomposition shows that movements in the Marxian rate of profit are driven by the rate of surplus value, while the organic composition of capital is stable, and that about three-fifths of surplus value is absorbed by the wages of unproductive labor.

Ongoing extensions: a long historical series based on input-output tables since 1997, and occupational data to separate productive and unproductive labor within industries.

Long-Run Profit Rate Dynamics: Empirical Evidence from the Chinese and U.S. Economies [PDF]

with Junshang Liang · Working paper, September 2026

Abstract

This paper examines the relationship between the organic composition of capital and the rate of profit in the United States and China. Rather than inferring the mechanism from trends or accounting decompositions, we study the dynamic relationship between the two variables, using vector error-correction methods for the U.S. nonfinancial corporate economy (1929–2022) and dynamic panel methods for Chinese industrial enterprises. In the United States, the two variables share a negative long-run relationship, but deviations from it are corrected through the composition of capital while the profit rate is weakly exogenous, implying that long-run causation runs from profitability to the composition of capital rather than the reverse. The Chinese evidence does not show a negative effect over the available sample period.

Ongoing extensions: measures of the composition of capital that are not mechanically linked to the measured profit rate, a larger U.S. system with structural breaks and a postwar sample, and a longer Chinese panel.

Distributions of Profit Rates in China [PDF]

Working paper, September 2026

Abstract

Classical political economy describes competition as a process that tends to equalize profit rates without making them uniform, so that competition produces a distribution of profit rates rather than a single rate. This paper studies that distribution for Chinese industrial enterprises in 1996–2007, comparing four maximum-entropy models: the exponential, Gamma, asymmetric Laplace, and quantal response statistical equilibrium (QRSE) distributions. The distribution is sharply peaked and asymmetric, and a substantial share of firms report losses. The asymmetric Laplace and QRSE models, which accommodate negative profit rates, describe the data more closely than the exponential and Gamma distributions. Over the period, the mean profit rate rises, the lower tail of the distribution contracts, and the upper tail lengthens.

Ongoing extensions: a formal model comparison on a common version of the data, and an application to U.S. firm-level profit rates and markups.
